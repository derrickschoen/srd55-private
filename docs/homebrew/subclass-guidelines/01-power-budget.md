Purpose: place each feature on the host class's measured schedule and size it according to how much power and identity that class delegates to its subclass.

# Per-class subclass power budget

## Central frame

A subclass does not receive a class-independent allowance. The base class, its spell progression, its native options, and its action economy determine what remains. Full casters usually have the least raw-power room because spellcasting already supplies scaling, choices, and new capabilities. A subclass may redirect that power, but should rarely stack a second engine on top.

The strongest predictor of subclass dependence is whether the base class already carries its own customization engine. A spellbook, a broad spell list, metamagic-like alteration, invocation-like picks, inspiration-like spending, or a complete aura-and-strike package already differentiates characters. Fighter has no comparable internal menu or resource system, so its subclass carries the greatest design burden and has the widest permission to supply a playstyle engine.

The previously similar-looking Rogue, Monk, and Ranger require separate treatment under the current SRD chassis. Monk and Ranger were strengthened at the base and now ask less of subclasses. Rogue still needs more help, but receives it at 3/9/13/17: the latest and sparsest effective progression for a class with that need. Rogue is therefore the special-care class. Its subclass must deliver a satisfying loop at 3 without front-loading a multiclass package, then remain alive through a six-level gap without trespassing on another class or overloading the Bonus Action.

## Measured subclass-feature schedule

This table is measured from the twelve subclasses in SRD 5.2.1. It is authoritative for these guidelines. There is no universal 3/6/10/14 schedule; only Barbarian, Druid, Warlock, and Wizard use it. `3×2` means two separately named features at class level 3, not two additional schedule slots.

| Host class | Subclass levels | Named-feature cadence in the SRD subclass | Named features |
|---|---|---|---:|
| Barbarian | 3 / 6 / 10 / 14 | 3×1, 6×1, 10×1, 14×1 | 4 |
| Bard | 3 / 6 / 14 | 3×2, 6×1, 14×1 | 4 |
| Cleric | 3 / 6 / 17 | 3×3, 6×1, 17×1 | 5 |
| Druid | 3 / 6 / 10 / 14 | 3×2, 6×1, 10×1, 14×1 | 5 |
| Fighter | 3 / 7 / 10 / 15 / 18 | 3×2, 7×1, 10×1, 15×1, 18×1 | 6 |
| Monk | 3 / 6 / 11 / 17 | 3×1, 6×1, 11×1, 17×1 | 4 |
| Paladin | 3 / 7 / 15 / 20 | 3×2, 7×1, 15×1, 20×1 | 5 |
| Ranger | 3 / 7 / 11 / 15 | 3×2, 7×1, 11×1, 15×1 | 5 |
| Rogue | 3 / 9 / 13 / 17 | 3×2, 9×1, 13×1, 17×1 | 5 |
| Sorcerer | 3 / 6 / 14 / 18 | 3×2, 6×1, 14×1, 18×1 | 5 |
| Warlock | 3 / 6 / 10 / 14 | 3×2, 6×1, 10×1, 14×1 | 5 |
| Wizard | 3 / 6 / 10 / 14 | 3×2, 6×1, 10×1, 14×1 | 5 |

Use the exact levels for placement, but use tiers for design intent:

| Tier | Class levels | Feature job |
|---|---:|---|
| Entry | 3–4 | Establish the repeatable identity loop without becoming the career's power peak. |
| Tier 2 | 5–10 | Improve reliability, combinations, or reach; this is often the most-played upgrade. |
| Tier 3 | 11–16 | Add a new verb, broader scene control, or faster use of the established engine. Raw numeric growth alone is insufficient. |
| Tier 4 | 17–20 | Deliver a large, simple culmination that assumes routine flight, long-range movement, powerful recovery, and crowded turns. |

When a class skips a tier, its next feature must bridge the experiential gap without containing two full features' worth of raw power. Bard and Cleric have only three schedule levels. Paladin's last feature arrives at 20. Rogue waits from 3 to 9. These facts demand efficient identity, not extra unofficial slots.

## Class dependence and power shape

`Dependence` describes how much play identity and outcome-changing weight the subclass must carry. It is not permission to exceed same-class SRD siblings.

| Class | Dependence | Why | Power the subclass should add | Damage share |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Fighter | **High** | The base has no broad customization engine; the subclass supplies the main playstyle and often its resource. | One coherent resource or mode, action interaction, and non-combat identity. | High, but prefer once-per-turn riders to extra attacks. |
| Rogue | **High by need; low by schedule** | The base needs reliability and differentiation, while the 3/9/13/17 schedule offers little room and long gaps. | Reliable access to the core precision-damage loop, careful action reshaping, and a distinct utility niche. | Medium; reliability is safer than a large new rider. |
| Cleric | **Medium-high identity; low raw headroom** | Only three schedule levels, but prepared subclass spells and a new use of the class's channelled resource can redirect play strongly. Full casting already supplies the power curve. | Fixed spell access plus one support or defensive mode tied to the class engine. | Low. Spend on healing, defense, support, or control. |
| Monk | **Medium** | The repaired base is functional on its own; the subclass should alter what its Focus buys rather than repair damage and defense again. | New Focus modes, movement, control, or utility attached to existing actions. | Medium-low; avoid stacking several damage and mitigation riders. |
| Ranger | **Medium** | The base now owns more exploration skill and combat infrastructure. | A core-mark interaction, bounded combat rider, and player-initiated exploration utility. | Medium-high, with a utility counterweight. |
| Druid | **Medium** | Full casting carries raw power, while the subclass can substantially redirect Wild Shape. | An alternative use or expression of Wild Shape and a utility identity. | Low. Avoid a second full combat engine beside spells. |
| Warlock | **Medium-low** | Spells and invocation choices already form a customization engine. | Patron spell access and one thematic mode that cooperates with Pact Magic. | Low-medium and frequency-limited. |
| Barbarian | **Medium-low** | The base state already fixes the main loop; subclasses have limited room to differentiate without merely adding damage. | One twist active during Rage plus defense, control, movement, or utility. | High proportionally, but bounded to the core state and once per turn. |
| Paladin | **Low-medium** | The base supplies strong strikes, healing, and auras; the subclass has few commonly reached slots. | Oath spell access, one Channel Divinity mode, and a restrained aura or protective pivot. | Low. Do not multiply the base burst. |
| Sorcerer | **Low** | Full casting, Sorcery Points, and spell alteration carry the engine. | Fixed spell access and one Sorcery Point interaction that changes casting style. | Low. Favor flexibility or transformation over more spell damage. |
| Bard | **Low** | Full casting, expertise, and Bardic Inspiration already cover power and identity; there are only three schedule levels. | A distinct use of Bardic Inspiration and a role-shaping utility. | Low. |
| Wizard | **Low** | The spellbook and spell list are the largest customization and capability engine. | Change how selected spells are cast; do not duplicate what spell selection already does. | Very low; spend chiefly on casting texture, safety, or specialization. |

Within full casters, `medium` dependence means the subclass can strongly redirect an existing resource; it does not mean the subclass should contribute martial-scale extra damage. Subclass identity and added raw power are separate budgets.

## Numbered budget heuristics

### 1. Design by tier; place by exact schedule

First decide what the feature must accomplish in entry play, tier 2, tier 3, or tier 4. Then put it at the host's measured level. A literal label such as *the level-10 feature* does not transfer between classes.

### 2. Put the playable loop at level 3

The first bundle must let the character perform the advertised behavior now. It may be incomplete, but cannot be a preview. Level 3 defines the engine; later levels improve its reliability, choices, scope, or action cost. Do not let level 3 remain the strongest point of the subclass.

### 3. Size entry damage riders as a share of the host's at-will turn

For a provisional entry-tier check, treat a once-per-turn rider contributing roughly one-quarter to one-third of an ordinary at-will turn as substantial. At a 65% hit chance, `1d6` on one eligible hit contributes about 2.28 expected damage. This range is a **constructed anchor, not a measured community norm or an SRD rule**. Recalculate against the actual host and its same-slot SRD sibling; do not promote the anchor into a universal allowance.

### 4. Measure rider decay at later tiers

A fixed rider becomes a smaller part of the turn as attacks, spell levels, ability modifiers, and class resources improve. Test the same rider at levels 5, 11, and 17. If it becomes decorative, scale the die at host-class levels or attach the effect to an engine that already scales. If it multiplies with attack count, limit it to once on each of the user's turns. The share targets are constructed diagnostic anchors, not observed norms.

### 5. Keep at-will and nova budgets separate

Record sustained output and best-round output in different columns. A feature can leave average damage modest while making a one-encounter day collapse under stacked burst. Conversely, a scarce flourish can look excessive when incorrectly averaged as if it occurred every turn. Test the best legal stack, the ordinary fifth use, and the no-resource turn.

### 6. Price a die added to a d20 roll with `(X + 1) / 40`

Away from automatic-failure, automatic-success, and boundary cases, adding `1dX` changes success probability by about `(X + 1) / 40`: d4 ≈ 12.5 percentage points, d6 ≈ 17.5, d8 ≈ 22.5. This is a mathematical estimate, not a guarantee; target numbers near the ends of the d20 range reduce its accuracy. A d20 rider is much more valuable than the same die added to damage.

### 7. Treat advantage as about `+3.3`, approximately a d6 d20 rider

Across common target numbers, advantage acts like roughly `+3.3`; for budgeting, compare it to adding about `1d6` to a d20 roll. This is an approximation and shifts with the target number. Either benefit needs a price: a resource, positioning requirement, narrow duration, or once-per-turn boundary. Advantage also increases critical frequency and magnifies other accuracy-dependent benefits.

### 8. Gate and de-stack flat bonuses

A flat modifier does not fail merely because it is flat. It fails when it is always present, frequent, and stackable on attack rolls, Armor Class, saving throws, or save DCs. Require a condition or limited use, state that duplicate instances do not stack where needed, and test it alongside cover, equipment, spells, and party support. Prefer non-accuracy value when the same fantasy can be expressed as damage, movement, temporary Hit Points, or utility.

### 9. Use the uses-per-rest ladder deliberately

As availability falls, each use may be stronger:

`at will` → `several uses per Long Rest` → `about once per Short Rest` → `once per Long Rest`.

This is a pricing ladder, not a recommendation to choose the rarest form. At-will effects must be modest. A long-rest pool is predictable and may receive a small short-rest top-up. A short-rest engine must survive a day with no Short Rest. A once-per-Long-Rest showpiece must be checked for hoarding and one-fight-day burst. Prefer reusing the class pool; the SRD baseline rarely creates a subclass-only counter.

### 10. Budget action-economy real estate before magnitude

Inventory the host class's Action, Bonus Action, Reaction, concentration, and attack riders. A new Bonus Action is costly for Rogue, Monk, Ranger, and Paladin because their core turns already compete for it. Reactions often have less mechanical competition but carry off-turn interruption cost. Free riders are powerful when they attach to every hit, spell, or attack; limit them before increasing their die. Extra actions or attacks are presumptive overshoots because they carry every existing rider with them.

### 11. Make the signature spend conscious; make support fast

A strong limited feature should normally ask the player whether to spend it. That choice makes scarcity meaningful and exposes usage in playtest. Supporting features can be static or automatic to preserve table speed. Avoid filling every turn with optional micro-riders; the subclass gets one recurring decision center.

### 12. Do not add a new ability-score dependency

Use an ability the base class already advances, the class's save DC, or a class-level value. A new required ability is not honest balancing; it makes characters who choose the subclass after creation discover that their earlier choices were traps. Never repair an overtuned feature by making it multi-ability dependent.

### 13. Treat four shapes as near-automatic overshoots

Stop and redesign unless the exception is proved against the whole chassis:

- doubled proficiency on attacks, broadly applicable checks, or saving throws;
- two broadly valuable saving-throw proficiencies in one grant;
- an unarmored-defense formula without a real multi-attribute or equipment tradeoff;
- a resource-recovery loop whose output can restore its own input.

Extra actions, unrestricted extra attacks, unconditional accuracy, and attack-stat substitution belong in the same high-scrutiny category.

### 14. Flank a strong slot with subdued neighbors

Judge the tier total, not each feature in isolation. If one slot must carry a large identity payoff, reduce adjacent combat weight and use those neighbors for defense, utility, or a ribbon. Never justify a weak early experience with a powerful late feature that many campaigns will not reach.

### 15. Compare within the same class and slot

Compare a draft only with SRD subclasses on the same parent chassis and, first, at the same schedule position. Cross-class feature matching ignores base spells, attacks, resources, and action congestion. After slot comparison, compare the entire subclass at each tier; individually acceptable borrowed shapes can form an excessive kit.

### 16. Set damage share per class

There is no universal percentage of subclass power that should be damage. Fighter and Barbarian can spend a large share on bounded damage because their chassis expects attacks to carry the turn. Ranger can spend a meaningful share but must also supply exploration identity. Rogue should favor precision-damage reliability over a separate burst engine. Monk should buy new Focus modes. Wizard, Cleric, Bard, Sorcerer, Druid, and Warlock should spend much less on raw damage because spellcasting already owns that axis. These shares are design posture, not measured percentages; prove the actual numbers by within-class comparison and playtest.

## Budget record for each drafted feature

For every feature, record: schedule slot and tier; rock or ribbon; at-will and nova effect; action and concentration cost; use cadence; expected rounds of relevance; scaling source; host-class interaction; multiclass export value; pillar served; and the same-class SRD feature used as the structural comparator. If any field is unknown, the feature is provisional.
