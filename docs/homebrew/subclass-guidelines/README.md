# What the subclass guidelines ended up saying

A report on the six clean-room files in this folder (D177). Written for the
owner after reading all six in full; every claim below is theirs, with the
file it comes from. The files were produced by isolated researcher agents
who were then retired; the six documents cite only each other and SRD
5.2.1, and an independent hygiene audit (zero 7-gram overlap with the SRD
corpus, all proper names SRD-verified) cleared them for commit.

## The one-paragraph version

A subclass has no budget of its own — it spends what its class left over.
The files measure exactly what the twelve SRD 5.2.1 subclasses actually do
(schedules, action costs, resource shapes, spell grants, word counts),
extract the design physics from those measurements, and then wrap the
whole thing in a process: state a fantasy with a verb, build one playable
loop at level 3, deepen that loop at every later slot, run sixteen named
failure checks, and fill a ~50-item pre-flight where "pass" requires
evidence, not confidence.

## 00 — Process: the spine

The seven-step workflow: state the fantasy → copy the budget header → draft
the level-3 loop → draft later slots by their *job*, not their mechanics →
write rules in SRD language → audit against the taxonomy and checklist →
produce a review packet. Two rules with teeth:

- The fantasy must be **"a [class] who [does a visible thing repeatedly]
  to [change a scene]"** — a concept that is only an adjective, damage
  type, or costume is rejected before any mechanics exist.
- The clean-room boundary is procedural: a design may cite only these six
  files and anchored SRD lines. (This is the rule that caught nothing being
  citable for EK when we started the monk — and forced a
  better subclass.)

## 01 — Power budget: the numbers

The two most load-bearing findings in the whole set:

**There is no universal 3/6/10/14 schedule.** Measured from the SRD: only
Barbarian, Druid, Warlock, and Wizard use it. Paladin runs 3/7/15/20,
Rogue 3/9/13/17, Monk 3/6/11/17, Fighter 3/7/10/15/18, Bard and Cleric
have only three slots. Features are designed by *tier job* (entry loop →
reliability → new verb → simple culmination) and placed on the host's real
schedule.

**Dependence is a spectrum, and it moved in 2024.** Fighter and Rogue need
the most from their subclass (Fighter has no internal customization
engine at all); Wizard, Bard, Sorcerer need the least (full casting IS the
engine). The surprise: **Monk and Ranger are now only Medium** — the
repaired 2024 bases carry themselves, so their subclasses should change
*what Focus/marks buy*, not re-repair damage. Rogue is the special-care
class: high need, sparsest schedule (3 then nothing until 9).

Sixteen numbered heuristics follow. The ones we use constantly: a d6 on a
d20 roll ≈ 17.5 percentage points and advantage ≈ +3.3 (so accuracy is
always more expensive than damage); a new Bonus Action is costly on Rogue/
Monk/Ranger/Paladin; never add a new required ability score; four shapes
are near-automatic overshoots (doubled proficiency, double save
proficiencies, free unarmored-defense formulas, self-refilling resource
loops); and every draft compares only against the same-class SRD sibling
at the same slot — never a cherry-picked donor from another class.

## 02 — Cadence and anatomy: what the corpus actually contains

Measured across all 58 named features of the twelve SRD subclasses:

- **76% of subclass features are passives or free riders** on an existing
  action. Only 5 features in the whole corpus use a Bonus Action, 3 an
  Action, 3 a Reaction. The first-party feel comes from strengthening what
  the class already does.
- **Reuse before invention:** 50 of 58 features have no private counter —
  they ride Rage, Focus, Channel Divinity, Bardic Inspiration, Wild Shape,
  Sorcery Points, or spell slots. Late showpieces reload by spending
  native currency (a slot, Sorcery Points), not by a bespoke pool.
- **Spell-grant math:** the four fixed-list subclasses converge on exactly
  ten always-prepared spells (+4 at 3, then +2 steps). A spell list is
  budget, not flavor.
- **Text budget:** the densest SRD subclass spends 552 rules words; the
  leanest 199. Champion has six features in 199 words — brevity is a
  first-party trait.

## 03 — Fun: the experience contract

- The identity loop must run **at level 3** in four beats: observe →
  choose → act → change. The subclass "becomes itself in the session it is
  chosen."
- **One recurring decision center** per subclass; support features are
  passive or single-trigger. Players remember choices, not modifiers — but
  every visible option costs table time even when unused.
- Resources must stay *alive* (variable spend, native-currency conversion,
  a retrainable menu — pick ONE shape). A lone once-per-day showpiece as
  the identity is banned: it hoards on long days and dumps on short ones.
- Whole-session presence: a subclass may lean into one pillar but must
  leave the player something to *initiate* in the other two. Ribbons are
  respected tools with two limits: never a whole level, and several
  together count as real power.
- Calibrate **slightly under** the SRD sibling — a little weak is fixable
  at the table; a high outlier multiplies through feats, items, and
  multiclassing before anyone notices. But protect the identity-bearing
  dial: if the fantasy is battlefield movement, balance by cutting damage
  or uses, never the movement.

## 04 — Failure taxonomy: sixteen ways a plausible draft dies

Each with recognition signs, why desk review misses it, and a repair.
The full list: F1 Compounding Punishment, F2 Imported Chassis Mismatch,
F3 Dip Bait, F4 Farmable/Famine Triggers, F5 Advantage Faucet, F6 Action
Congestion, F7 Runaway Interaction Math, F8 Stacking Blindness, F9
Golden-Cage Benchmark, F10 White-Room Day, F11 Campaign-Contingent
Payload, F12 Niche Trespass, F13 Bookkeeping Tax, F14 Bounced Flavor
Cheque, F15 Dead-Air Progression, F16 Untested Altitude.

The cross-cutting laws matter as much as the entries:

- **Frequency of relevance is the master variable** — a modest effect used
  every fight outweighs a dramatic one seen once a campaign; record
  eligible/noticed/offered/chosen for everything.
- **A drawback an item can erase is not a drawback** — it only taxes the
  player who honors the fiction.
- Two failures (F11, F12) are **social**, not numeric: a campaign must
  promise the scenes and the party must accept the overlap — mandate the
  table conversation instead of pretending arithmetic solves it.
- Review every kit twice: once as the optimizer, once from the seat of the
  ally or niche-holder it might harm.

## 05 — The pre-flight: where confidence goes to die

Ten sections, ~50 checkboxes, every one demanding an evidence blank: a
calculation, an SRD comparison, or a play result — "confidence alone is
not evidence." Notable teeth: the outsider audit must be run as **three
different level-17 multiclass shoppers**; the table-speed test is "say the
turn aloud and time the fifth use, not the first"; the bookkeeping cap is
**one tracked quantity, named**; and the release gate re-checks the
clean-room boundary (citations, names, quotations) one last time. Verdicts
are three-valued: ready for playtest / ready for design review only / not
ready. Unknowns become a provisional-risk table with an owner — never a
silent "it's probably fine."

## How it's working in practice

Two designs have run through these files so far. The Oath of Domination
(paladin) shipped its draft with the 05 checklist filled and its untested
tiers honestly amber. The monk third-caster catalog used 01's dependence
table to reject "monk needs a power infusion" (a supervisor error both
panel agents caught against the file), 02's free-rider baseline to prefer
strike-riding deliveries, and 04's F3/F6/F7 to annotate every pitch with
its most likely death. The files are doing the job they were written for:
they argue back.
